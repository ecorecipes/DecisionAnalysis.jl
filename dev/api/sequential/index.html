Designs, waiting and sequential decisions

What to observe, whether to wait for it, and how to manage when the decision is taken again and again. All of these are built on the definitions of Experiments and exact value of information.

Profiles and designs

DecisionAnalysis.voi_profile — Function
voi_profile(problem::DecisionProblem, experiment::Experiment; atol=0, rtol) -> NamedTuple

The value of an experiment outcome by outcome (Akinlotan et al. 2024): the expected value of sample information hides how the realised value varies over what the study might show. For each signal z of positive probability the profile gives

  • probability: P(z) = Σ_θ p(θ) L[z, θ];
  • best: the actions that are optimal after z (ties as in decide);
  • posterior_value: the expected utility of the best action after z;
  • value: the value of the information in this outcome, the posterior expected utility of the best action minus that of the act-now action a* (the first Bayes action under the prior). It is never negative, and zero exactly when a* is still optimal after z;
  • changes_decision: whether a* is no longer optimal after z.

The result is (; act_now_action, outcomes, evsi, variance, standard_deviation, probability_zero): evsi = Σ_z P(z) value_z is evsi(problem, experiment) exactly; variance and standard_deviation are those of the per-outcome value over the signals; probability_zero is the probability that the study changes nothing. A study with a large EVSI that comes from a rare outcome has a large standard deviation and a probability_zero near one, which matters to a risk-averse manager. A signal of probability zero is listed with value zero and no posterior. Exact in, exact out, except the standard deviation. Needs a single prior.

julia> p = DecisionProblem(PayoffTable([0 -100; -30 -30]), [3 // 5, 2 // 5]);

julia> profile = voi_profile(p, Experiment([4//5 1//5; 1//5 4//5]));

julia> [(o.signal, o.probability, o.value) for o in profile.outcomes]
2-element Vector{Tuple{Symbol, Rational{Int64}, Rational{Int64}}}:
 (:z1, 14//25, 110//7)
 (:z2, 11//25, 0)

julia> profile.evsi, profile.probability_zero
(44//5, 11//25)

DecisionAnalysis.expected_variance_reduction — Function
expected_variance_reduction(problem, experiment::Experiment, quantity)
expected_variance_reduction(problem, partition::AbstractVector, quantity)
expected_variance_reduction(problem, focal; quantity, method=nothing)

The value of information for estimation: by how much, on average, a study reduces the variance of a quantity of interest (Jackson et al. 2019). quantity has one number per state (the abundance in that state, a parameter, the payoff of one action). With g the quantity,

reduction = Var(g) − E_z Var(g | z) = Var(E[g | z]),

which is never negative. The result is (; prior_variance, expected_posterior_variance, reduction, share) with share = reduction / prior_variance. It measures learning, not deciding: a study can remove most of the variance of a quantity and still have an evsi of zero, when no outcome would change the action, so read the two side by side.

  • With an Experiment: the reduction from observing its signal. Exact.
  • With a partition of the states (as for evppi): the reduction from learning the cell. Exact.
  • With a SampleBelief and focal (a parameter name or a tuple of names): the reduction from learning those parameters, estimated as the weighted variance of the fitted conditional mean of the quantity given them, with method as for evppi (default: chosen by the focal parameters). quantity is a vector with one number per draw, or the name of a parameter of the draws.

Units are those of the quantity squared (individuals squared for a count). Needs a single prior.

julia> p = DecisionProblem(PayoffTable([0 -100; -30 -30]), [3 // 5, 2 // 5]);

julia> r = expected_variance_reduction(p, Experiment([4//5 1//5; 1//5 4//5]), [10, 60]);

julia> r.prior_variance, r.reduction, r.share
(600//1, 16200//77, 27//77)

expected_variance_reduction(belief, focal; quantity, method=nothing)
expected_variance_reduction(belief, study; quantity)

The value of information for estimation from a SampleBelief alone, with no decision and no payoff table: a model that estimates a quantity (a growth rate, an abundance, a prevalence) from uncertain inputs. belief is the sample (or a sample problem, whose belief is used); quantity is a vector with one number per draw, the model's output at that draw, or the name of a column.

With focal this is the EVPPI for estimation, the expected reduction in the variance of the quantity from learning those parameters exactly, Var(E[quantity | focal]) (Jackson et al. 2019; evppivar of the R package voi). With a study it is the EVSI for estimation (evsivar), the reduction expected from the study's data, Var(quantity) − E Var(quantity | data):

  • SummaryStatistics: the variance of the regression of the quantity on the summaries of one simulated data set per draw;
  • ImportanceSampling: the variance, over the simulated data sets, of the posterior mean of the quantity;
  • GaussianApproximation: the variance explained by its focal parameters times n / (n0 + n); with a vector of sample sizes, a vector of results.

The result is as for the other methods, (; prior_variance, expected_posterior_variance, reduction, share). Under squared-error loss the reduction is the value of the information, in the units of the quantity squared; a weighted sum of several outputs is one quantity, quantity = c₁ g₁ + c₂ g₂.

As in evppi, a regression is not fitted on a focal parameter that is constant or a linear function of the ones named before it.

DecisionAnalysis.optimal_design — Function
optimal_design(problem::DecisionProblem, designs; costs=nothing, population=1,
               seed=1) -> NamedTuple

Compare a finite list of candidate studies and name the best. designs is a named collection (a named tuple, a dictionary or name => design pairs) of

  • Experiments, valued exactly by evsi on a finite problem, or
  • studies of a sample problem (SummaryStatistics, ImportanceSampling, GaussianApproximation), valued by the sample-based evsi, each with its standard error;

or a single GaussianApproximation with a vector of sample sizes, which is compared across its sizes (designs named n = 10, ...).

Each design is scored by its expected net benefit of sampling, population · EVSI − cost. costs gives the cost of each design in individuals (animals removed or killed by the sampling, say): nothing uses the cost of each experiment and zero for the studies; otherwise a vector in the order of the designs, a named collection, or a function of the sample size (of the name, for designs without one). There are no monetary costs in this package, and a net benefit is meaningful only for additive payoffs, as for enbs. When every cost is zero the comparison is by EVSI alone, and the most informative design wins.

The result is (; designs, best, tied, criterion, population): designs holds one (; name, evsi, standard_error, cost, net, net_standard_error) per design (standard errors nothing for exact values); best is the name of the design with the largest net benefit, the first in the order given when several tie (tied lists them all); criterion is :net_benefit or :evsi.

With estimated EVSIs the best design is picked from noisy numbers, so its reported net benefit overstates on average what it is worth (the optimiser's curse, as for the act-now value): read the standard errors, and prefer the smaller study when two are within them.

julia> p = DecisionProblem(PayoffTable([0 -100; -30 -30]), [3 // 5, 2 // 5]);

julia> coarse = Experiment([4//5 1//5; 1//5 4//5]; cost=2);

julia> fine = Experiment([19//20 1//20; 1//20 19//20]; cost=8);

julia> d = optimal_design(p, (; coarse, fine)); (d.best, [x.net for x in d.designs])
(:fine, Rational{Int64}[34//5, 77//10])

Waiting, and surveying before acting

DecisionAnalysis.value_of_waiting — Function
value_of_waiting(problem::DecisionProblem, delayed::PayoffTable, experiment::Experiment)
value_of_waiting(problem::DecisionProblem, options) -> NamedTuple

Act now, or wait, learn, and act late? problem holds the payoffs of acting now. delayed holds the payoffs of taking each action late, after the wait, for the same actions and states and on the same outcome horizon (the deaths, animals or money of the whole period, the wait included), so that the damage done while waiting is in the table and is not a fee subtracted afterwards. experiment is what the wait reveals. With V0 the value of acting now (act_now_value),

value = Σ_z max_a Σ_θ p(θ) L[z, θ] Q_late[a, θ] − V0,

which can be negative, and waiting is chosen (wait) when it is strictly positive. It splits into what the information is worth to the late decision and what the delay costs with nothing learned (Lean: valueOfWaiting_eq):

value = evsi_delayed − delay_cost,
delay_cost = V0 − max_a Σ_θ p(θ) Q_late[a, θ].

The result is (; value, wait, act_now_value, act_now_action, waiting_value, delay_cost, evsi_delayed, evsi_now, late_action, policy). evsi_now is evsi(problem, experiment), the value of the same information if it arrived at once: when no action pays more late than now in any state, value ≤ evsi_now (Lean: valueOfWaiting_le_evsi), so a study that looks worth running on the payoffs of acting now may not be worth waiting for. late_action is the best late action with nothing learned, and policy gives the best late action after each signal of positive probability (the first on a tie). Infeasible actions are left out, now and late. Exact in, exact out. Needs a single prior.

With options, a collection of name => (delayed, experiment) pairs (several lengths of wait, each with its payoffs and what it reveals), the result is (; act_now_value, act_now_action, options, values, results, best): best is the name with the largest value, the first on a tie, or :act_now when no value is positive.

julia> now = PayoffTable([:none, :cull], [:mild, :severe], [0 -100; -30 -30]);

julia> late = PayoffTable([:none, :cull], [:mild, :severe], [0 -100; -40 -55]);

julia> p = DecisionProblem(now, [3 // 5, 2 // 5]; baseline=:none);

julia> w = value_of_waiting(p, late, Experiment([1 0; 0 1]; signals=[:mild, :severe]));

julia> w.value, w.wait, w.evsi_delayed, w.delay_cost, w.evsi_now
(8//1, true, 18//1, 10//1, 18//1)

julia> w.policy
2-element Vector{Pair{Symbol, Symbol}}:
   :mild => :none
 :severe => :cull

DecisionAnalysis.survey_or_act — Function
survey_or_act(problem::DecisionProblem, experiment::Experiment;
              action_costs, survey_cost, policy=nothing) -> NamedTuple

For one management unit: act now, or survey first and act on what the survey shows, when the actions and the survey both cost money and the question is what each unit of money buys (Raymond et al. 2020). The problem needs a baseline (no intervention) and a single prior; values are expected gains over the baseline, as in expected_gain_over_baseline.

  • action_costs: the cost of each action over and above the baseline, as a vector in the order of the actions of the payoff table or as action => cost pairs. The baseline costs zero and every other feasible action costs more than zero.
  • survey_cost: the cost of the survey, more than zero, paid whatever it shows.

Two options are compared.

  • Act now with action a: value Σ_θ p(θ) (Q[a, θ] − Q[baseline, θ]) at cost action_costs[a]. The action reported is the one with the largest value per unit of cost (the first of them on a tie).
  • Survey first with a policy z ↦ a_z: value Σ_z Σ_θ p(θ) L[z, θ] (Q[a_z, θ] − Q[baseline, θ]) at the expected cost survey_cost + Σ_z P(z) action_costs[a_z]. With policy=nothing the policy is the one with the largest value per unit of expected cost over all policies; policy given as signal => action pairs imposes one (Raymond et al. buy a unit only where the species is found).

The unit is surveyed first when that option has strictly more value per unit of cost; on a tie it is acted on now. The result is (; act_now, survey, survey_first, choice, value, cost, largest_cost, cost_effectiveness): act_now is (; action, value, cost, cost_effectiveness); survey is (; policy, value, cost, largest_cost, probability_of_acting, cost_effectiveness), where cost is the expected cost, largest_cost the cost if the survey returns its most expensive outcome, and probability_of_acting the probability that the survey is followed by an action other than the baseline; choice is :act_now or :survey, and the last four fields are those of the chosen option. Exact in, exact out.

Surveying and then taking the same action whatever is seen has the value of acting now at a higher cost, so a survey is chosen only when some outcome leads to a cheaper action: its worth here is the money it saves on units where acting would gain little, which a budget can then spend elsewhere (select_under_budget). This is not evsi, which values a study when the costs are already in the payoffs.

For one action of value 1 where a species is present (prior p), cost C, and a survey of cost M that finds a present species with probability d and never finds an absent one, the unit is surveyed first exactly when d C (1 − p) > M (Lean: survey_first_iff).

julia> t = PayoffTable([:none, :protect], [:absent, :present], [0 0; 0 1]);

julia> p = DecisionProblem(t, [9 // 10, 1 // 10]; baseline=:none);

julia> e = Experiment([1 3//10; 0 7//10]; signals=[:not_found, :found]);

julia> u = survey_or_act(p, e; action_costs=[0, 5000], survey_cost=750);

julia> u.choice, u.survey.policy
(:survey, [:not_found => :none, :found => :protect])

julia> u.act_now.value, u.act_now.cost, u.survey.value, u.survey.cost
(1//10, 5000, 7//100, 1100//1)

DecisionAnalysis.select_under_budget — Function
select_under_budget(units, budget; option=:chosen, passes=nothing,
                    method=:greedy) -> NamedTuple

Choose which management units to work on with a fixed budget, from the results of survey_or_act (Raymond et al. 2020). Each unit enters with one option: the one it chose (option=:chosen), acting now (:act_now) or surveying first (:survey); the last two give the "never survey" and "always survey" comparisons.

  • method = :greedy (default): Raymond et al. (2020)'s ranking heuristic by value per unit of cost, with optional passes cap (passes = nothing runs until no unit fits; passes = 2 matches the printed supplement listing).
  • method = :exact: exact 0–1 knapsack optimisation via branch-and-bound in exact arithmetic (no external solver required), returning the provably value-maximising subset within budget (in index order; passes = 1). For a JuMP formulation or LP relaxation, see budget_selection_model.

The result is (; selected, choices, value, cost, largest_cost, remaining, passes): the indices of the units taken; the option of each; the total value; the total cost; the total if every survey returned its most expensive outcome; the budget left; and the number of passes made.

julia> units = [(; choice=:act_now, value=v, cost=c, largest_cost=c)
                for (v, c) in [(6, 60), (5, 55), (1, 20), (1, 30)]];

julia> s = select_under_budget(units, 100);

julia> s.selected, s.value, s.cost, s.remaining
([1, 3], 7, 80, 20)

Decisions repeated over time

DecisionAnalysis.adaptive_management — Function
adaptive_management(problem::DecisionProblem, experiments;
                    horizon, discount=1, depth=min(horizon, 3),
                    transitions=nothing, signal_of=:current,
                    method=:beliefs, passive=(method !== :points),
                    grid=10) -> NamedTuple

Sequential (adaptive) management: the decision of problem is taken in each of horizon periods. The state is unknown, and fixed unless transitions is given; in each period the action a pays Q[a, θ] and reveals a signal, the belief is updated, and the next action is chosen on the new belief. What is learned depends on what is done:

  • experiments is one Experiment, observed whatever the action, or a collection of action => Experiment pairs (a vector of pairs, a named tuple or a dictionary). An action that is not named reveals nothing.

  • discount multiplies the payoffs of each later period (δ ≥ 0; period t has weight δ^(t−1)).

  • transitions makes the state move between periods. It is one matrix, used whatever the action, or a collection of action => matrix pairs naming every feasible action. A matrix has T[θ′, θ] = P(next state θ′ | present state θ): rows are the next state and columns the present one, so every column sums to one, as in an Experiment. The prior is the belief about the state of the first period.

  • signal_of says which state the signal of a period is about: :current (default), the state that the period's payoff came from, which then moves; or :next, the state after the move (the usual convention of partially observed Markov decision processes). The two coincide when the state is fixed.

  • method is how the best policy is found; both are exact. :beliefs (default) evaluates every belief that can be reached, whose number grows with every period. :vectors keeps the value function as the maximum of a set of linear functions of the belief, one set for each number of periods left, and discards the functions that are never the maximum. The sets are usually far smaller than the set of beliefs, so long horizons become practical. It is available for two states only, where the functions are lines and the ones that matter are found by sorting.

  • method=:points is for any number of states over long horizons, and is not exact: it returns two numbers that the value of the best policy lies between. active is a lower bound, the value of a policy that can be carried out: the value function is backed up only at the beliefs of a grid (entries that are multiples of 1 / grid, and the prior). active_upper is an upper bound, from the same grid, using that the value function is convex. Both are computed exactly for exact inputs; only the grid is an approximation, and the gap between the two says how good it is. A finer grid narrows it and costs more: the number of points is binomial(grid + n − 1, n − 1) for n states. With this method perfect and baseline are exact. The fixed plan (no_learning, plan, act_now_action) is found, exactly, only where the state is fixed or moves in the same way whatever is done; otherwise those fields, value_of_learning and evpi are nothing.

  • passive=false leaves out the passive manager. Its value is not a maximum of linear functions, so with every method it is found by following its beliefs, and where those multiply it can take longer than everything else. It is left out by default with method=:points, where asking for it throws an ArgumentError unless the fixed plan can be found (see above).

Four ways of managing are valued, each as a total over the horizon:

  • no_learning: the best plan fixed in advance, which ignores what is seen. With a fixed state that is the best single action on the prior, repeated; with transitions the plan allows for how the state is expected to move, and may change action from period to period;
  • passive: in each period the action that would be best if nothing more were going to be learned, with the belief updated as results arrive, but no action ever taken in order to learn (passive adaptive management). With a fixed state that is the best action for the current belief; with transitions it is the first action of the best fixed plan for the remaining periods, made again after each signal;
  • active: the best policy, which may take an action that pays less now because of what it reveals (active adaptive management). It is found by the recursion V_n(w) = max_a [Σ_θ w(θ) Q[a, θ] + δ Σ_z V_{n−1}(w′_{a,z})], with w′_{a,z} = w · L_a[z, ·] for a fixed state, T_a (w · L_a[z, ·]) with transitions, and (T_a w) · L_a[z, ·] with signal_of=:next;
  • perfect: the state known. With transitions, the state is seen before every action (the fully observed Markov decision process), not only at the start.

Always no_learning ≤ passive ≤ active ≤ perfect (proved in the Lean development, Finite/Adaptive.lean and, for a state that moves, Finite/AdaptiveDynamic.lean). With a moving state a manager who took only the best action for the current period could do worse than the fixed plan, which is why passive plans ahead. The result is (; horizon, discount, no_learning, passive, active, active_upper, perfect, value_of_learning, value_of_probing, evpi, baseline, act_now_action, first_action, plan, policy, beliefs_evaluated, value_function): value_of_learning = active − no_learning; value_of_probing = active − passive, what deliberate experimentation adds; evpi = perfect − no_learning, which bounds both; baseline is the value of taking the baseline action in every period (nothing without a baseline); act_now_action is the first action of the passive and no-learning managers and first_action that of the active one, so the two differ exactly when it pays to probe at once. plan is the fixed plan of the no-learning manager, one action for each period. With passive=false, passive and value_of_probing are nothing.

active_upper equals active with the exact methods.

value_function is nothing with method=:beliefs. With :vectors (and with :points, where it gives the lower bound) its entry n is (; actions, vectors) for n periods left: the value at the belief b is maximum(vectors * b), and the action of the row that attains it is a best action there. So the best action at any belief and any time can be read off without running the recursion again. (first_action and the policy tree break ties towards the first action; the rows of value_function need not.)

policy is the active policy as a tree, to depth periods: each node is (; period, probability, belief, action, value, branches), with the probability of reaching it, the belief there, the action taken, the expected value from there on given that the node is reached, and branches, a vector of signal => node for the signals of positive probability. branches is empty at the last period shown, and after an action that shows nothing, where the tree stops. policy is nothing with depth=0.

Ties go to the first action. Exact in, exact out. With method=:beliefs the recursion visits every distinct belief once (beliefs_evaluated, which is nothing with the other methods); beliefs reached by the same observations in a different order coincide, so repeated trials of one kind stay cheap, but the number grows quickly with the number of signals, actions and periods. With floating-point inputs, beliefs that agree to twelve significant digits are treated as one. The exact weights are products of one likelihood per period, so over many periods rational inputs should be Rational{BigInt} (machine rationals overflow with an error). Needs a single prior; infeasible actions are left out. Payoffs, experiments and transitions are the same in every period.

julia> t = PayoffTable([:safe, :risky], [:bad, :good], [0 0; -3//2 1]);

julia> p = DecisionProblem(t, [1 // 2, 1 // 2]; baseline=:safe);

julia> shows = Experiment([1 0; 0 1]; signals=[:failed, :worked]);

julia> r = adaptive_management(p, (; risky=shows); horizon=3);

julia> r.no_learning, r.passive, r.active, r.perfect
(0//1, 0//1, 3//4, 3//2)

julia> r.act_now_action, r.first_action, r.value_of_probing
(:safe, :risky, 3//4)

julia> [signal => node.action for (signal, node) in r.policy.branches]
2-element Vector{Pair{Symbol, Symbol}}:
 :failed => :safe
 :worked => :risky
