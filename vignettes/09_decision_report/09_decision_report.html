Motivation

The earlier vignettes computed one thing at a time. A decision-maker needs them together: what each rule recommends, how each action compares with doing nothing, how much could be regretted, what information would be worth, and under which assumptions all of it holds. DecisionReport assembles that for one problem. Its purpose is to make a contested decision look contested, and an easy one look easy.

The problem

The frog pond once more. Bd may be absent, at low prevalence or at high prevalence, and the manager can do nothing, give antifungal baths, or drain and disinfect the pond. Utility is uninfected frogs minus w times infected frogs, two years from now. Infected but living frogs are not counted as population: at w = 0 they are ignored, and at w = 1 each cancels one uninfected frog. The report is built at w = 1 and compared with w = 0 at the end.

All numbers are illustrative, not data.

using DecisionAnalysis
using Tables
include(joinpath("..", "_common.jl"))

actions = [:no_intervention, :antifungal_baths, :disinfect_pond]
states = [:absent, :low_prevalence, :high_prevalence]
uninfected = [500 300 100; 470 380 250; 350 340 320]
infected = [0 150 250; 0 60 120; 0 10 20]
prior = FiniteBelief(states, [1 // 2, 3 // 10, 1 // 5])

function bd_problem(w)
    return DecisionProblem(PayoffTable(actions, states, uninfected .- w .* infected),
                           prior; baseline=:no_intervention)
end

problem = bd_problem(1)
payoff_markdown(problem.payoff; corner="uninfected − infected")

Three further ingredients are optional. A credal set says how far the prior might be wrong: here the stated prior and two alternatives, one more hopeful and one less. An experiment is the swab test. The Hurwicz index is fixed at 1/2.

credal = CredalSet(states, [[1 // 2, 3 // 10, 1 // 5],
                            [7 // 10, 1 // 5, 1 // 10],
                            [3 // 10, 3 // 10, 2 // 5]])
swab = Experiment([19//20 2//5 1//20; 1//20 3//5 19//20];
                  signals=[:negative, :positive], states=states)

Assumptions
  1. Payoffs are conditional expected utilities of each action in each state; higher is better.
  2. State-wise rules (maximin, maximax, Laplace, Hurwicz, minimax regret) ignore probabilities and range over every state of the table, including any of probability zero.
  3. Regret is measured against the best feasible action in each state, so it changes when the set of actions changes.
  4. Tied actions are all reported; the first choice is the tied action that comes first in the payoff table.
  5. Expected utility, expected regret and EVPI are under the single stated prior; EVPI is the value of learning the state exactly and at no cost.
  6. EVSI is gross of the cost of the experiment and assumes the stated likelihood.
  7. The credal set is the convex hull of 3 stated priors; E-admissibility is decided over the whole hull.
  8. The rule-specific robust EVPI is under Γ-maximin; the envelope is the range of the single-prior EVPI over the credal set.
  9. The baseline (no intervention) is no_intervention; gains are payoffs minus the payoff of the baseline in the same state.
  10. All payoffs are illustrative.
  11. Utility is uninfected minus infected frogs (w = 1); infected but living frogs are not counted as population.

Reading it from the top:

  • Scores by rule. One column per rule, with a star on every chosen action. The minimax-regret column is a loss, so its star is on the smallest number.
  • First choice by rule. The single choice of each rule, and whether it is the baseline. Here maximax, which looks only at the best cell of each row, recommends doing nothing, and no other rule does.
  • Expected gain over the baseline. What each action adds to no intervention, in expectation under the stated prior.
  • Regret profile. Maximum and expected regret by action.
  • Robust sets. The actions that the credal set cannot rule out.
  • Value of information. EVPI and the EVSI of the swab under the stated prior, the EVPI of a Γ-maximin decision-maker, and the range of EVPI over the credal set.
  • Assumptions. The conditions under which the numbers hold, including the two supplied above.

On this problem the rules split three ways. Expected utility under the stated prior favours the baths. Maximin, Laplace, Hurwicz at 1/2, minimax regret and Γ-maximin over the three priors favour disinfection. Maximax favours doing nothing. The baths and disinfection are both E-admissible, and doing nothing is not. The report does not settle the choice between them. It shows that the choice turns on how much weight the bad states get, and that information is worth having: EVPI is about a seventh of the expected utility of acting now.

A Tables.jl table

A report is a Tables.jl row table with one row per feasible action, so it can be passed to anything that accepts a table: a DataFrame, a CSV writer, a plotting package.

What the report leaves out when it cannot be computed

The content depends on the belief. With a scenario set there are no probabilities, so the report has the state-wise rules and the maximum regret, and no expected utility, no expected gain and no value of information.

scenario_report = DecisionReport(DecisionProblem(problem.payoff, ScenarioSet(states);
                                                 baseline=:no_intervention))

Assumptions
  1. Payoffs are conditional expected utilities of each action in each state; higher is better.
  2. State-wise rules (maximin, maximax, Laplace, Hurwicz, minimax regret) ignore probabilities and range over the states of the scenario set.
  3. Regret is measured against the best feasible action in each state, so it changes when the set of actions changes.
  4. Tied actions are all reported; the first choice is the tied action that comes first in the payoff table.
  5. The baseline (no intervention) is no_intervention; gains are payoffs minus the payoff of the baseline in the same state, and no expected gain is shown because the belief has no single prior.

With a credal set as the belief of the problem itself, the rules that need one prior are left out and the robust rules are kept.

credal_report = DecisionReport(DecisionProblem(problem.payoff, credal;
                                               baseline=:no_intervention))
[string(r.rule) for r in credal_report.results],
credal_report.evpi === nothing, credal_report.expected_gain === nothing

At w = 0 disinfection has a negative expected gain: it is worse than doing nothing when the infected frogs it prevents are not counted. At w = 1 it gains 69. A report is a statement about one utility, and the weight belongs among its assumptions.

A feasibility filter in a report

Infeasible actions are left out of every part of a report, and the assumptions say so. If the pond cannot be drained this year:

restricted = DecisionProblem(problem.payoff, prior; baseline=:no_intervention,
                             feasible=[:no_intervention, :antifungal_baths])
r = DecisionReport(restricted)
r.evpi, last(r.assumptions, 3)
(15//1, ["Infeasible actions are excluded throughout: disinfect_pond.", "Expected utility, expected regret and EVPI are under the single stated prior; EVPI is the value of learning the state exactly and at no cost.", "The baseline (no intervention) is no_intervention; gains are payoffs minus the payoff of the baseline in the same state."])

Removing an action lowers EVPI here, because there is less to choose between once the state is known.

What to take away

  • DecisionReport(problem; credal, experiment, hurwicz, assumptions) puts every applicable rule side by side with the regret profile, the gain of each action over the baseline, the robust sets and the value of information.
  • It states its assumptions, and takes more through the assumptions keyword. Put the utility there, including the weight on infected animals and the fact that the numbers are illustrative.
  • It says for every rule whether the recommendation is the no-intervention baseline.
  • It is a Tables.jl table with one row per feasible action.
  • Parts that are not defined for the belief are left out, not guessed.
